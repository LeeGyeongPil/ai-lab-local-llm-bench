A kitchen is a good way to think about a language model server. The order slip has to be read before anything is cooked, and that reading takes longer when the slip is long. Once the cook starts, the plates leave the pass one at a time at a steady pace, no matter how long the slip was. If there is only one cook, a second customer simply waits in line, and the line grows with every new order while the pace of the plates stays the same. Before the first order of the day, the cook also has to fetch the ingredients from the storeroom, which is why the very first plate is always slow. A careful measurement therefore separates the waiting time, the fetching time, the reading time and the plating time instead of reporting one big number. Smaller recipes, written with fewer digits of precision, take less shelf space and are often a little quicker to prepare, though the dish can taste slightly different. Repeating the same order twice lets the cook reuse the notes from the first time, so a fair test changes the wording of every order. Writing down the date, the software version and the machine used makes the numbers useful to the next person who reads them.
